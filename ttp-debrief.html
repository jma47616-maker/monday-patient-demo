A low platelet count, then TTP

About 15 minutes.17 screens

20:10 · majors, bay 61 of 17

She isn't making sense.

  • Lauren Hale, 32, brought in by her partner. Twenty-four hours of worsening confusion. "Tonight she asked me whose house we were in."
  • Three days ago she saw blood in her urine. Her GP gave three days of nitrofurantoin for a urine infection.
  • Pulse 98, blood pressure 123/84, temperature 37.9 °C, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 14. Very pale.
  • Then the full blood count. Haemoglobin 72 g/L. Platelets 18. Eighteen months ago they were 268.
  • This page starts with that number: how to work up any low platelet count on the take. Then it follows hers to the diagnosis, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), and the first eight hours.
The decision: do you believe the number?2 of 17

First question for any low platelet count: is it real?

  • Normal is 150 to 450 × 10⁹/L. Below 150 is thrombocytopenia.
  • Platelets can clump in the sample tube after the blood is taken. The trigger is EDTA, the anticoagulant in the full blood count tube. The analyser undercounts, and the patient is fine. It happens in about 1 sample in 1,000.
  • Three quick checks: the previous counts, a repeat, and a blood film, which shows whether the platelets are there.
  • Lauren: 268 eighteen months ago, and the laboratory's words on her film were "Marked thrombocytopenia, confirmed on the film — not a clumping artefact". Real, and new.
The decision: where do you look for the cause?3 of 17

A real low count has only three explanations.

Tap each part.

  • Not made. The marrow is failing or crowded out: leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma, aplastic anaemia. Also B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, chemotherapy and viral infection.
  • Destroyed or used up. Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). Drugs, heparin and quinine among them. Infection. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). And the thrombotic microangiopathies: TTP and haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS).
  • In the wrong place, or diluted. About a third of platelets normally sit in the spleen. A big spleen, or portal hypertension from liver disease, holds more. Large transfusions dilute what is left.
  • History and examination sort most of this. Lauren gave little away: the mini pill, three days of nitrofurantoin, a negative pregnancy test, no nodes, no liver, no spleen.
The decision: who do you ring tonight?4 of 17

The number tells you about bleeding. It does not tell you the cause.

  • Above 50, most people have no symptoms. Spontaneous bleeding is uncommon until the count is below 20. Below 10 the risk is high.
  • Ring haematology the same day if the count is below 20, if the patient is bleeding or clotting, or if the count does not travel alone: new neurological symptoms, kidney injury, or haemolysis on the film.
  • Lauren had two before anyone looked at her film: 18, and confusion.
  • One warning. The ladder holds only when the problem is too few platelets. In one group of diseases a low count means the opposite of bleeding.
The decision: do you say yes?5 of 17

The blood bank is on the phone.

  • You sent a group and save. Two units of red cells are ready. Then the blood bank asks whether you also want platelets: "most people ask, with a count of 18."
  • Platelets 18. Haemoglobin 72. Her gums bled yesterday, and there are little marks on her arms and legs.
  • What do you say?
  • Not yet. One test first. A low platelet count beside an anaemia is not a platelet problem until the blood film says so.
  • If it shows fragments, this may be TTP, where platelet transfusion is associated with a significant increase in mortality. The reflex that suits most low counts is wrong here.
  • So: is it real, is it alone, and if not, look at the film.
The decision: what are you asking the laboratory to look for?6 of 17

One phone call to the laboratory decides the next step.

Three red cells, three different diseases. Tap each.

  • Fragments (schistocytes). Red cells torn as they are forced through damaged small vessels. This is microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). It is seen in TTP, HUS and DIC.
  • Spherocytes. Autoimmune haemolysis, or hereditary spherocytosis.
  • Bite cells and Heinz bodies. Oxidative haemolysis in G6PD deficiency (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, a red cell enzyme). This is the haemolysis nitrofurantoin can cause.
  • Lauren's film, phoned through: "Numerous schistocytes (red cell fragments)", polychromasia, no blasts. Her G6PD was normal.
  • Nitrofurantoin was a fair suspect: it can cause haemolysis and can lower platelets. It does not give you fragments.

20:25 · a urine sample, collected in majors8 of 17

The "urine infection" was explained on one slip of paper.

  • Three days ago, in her partner's words: "She saw blood in her water, that was all. No stinging, no going all the time, no temperature at home."
  • Tonight's slip: dark brown, "like strong tea". Blood +++. Urobilinogen raised. Leucocytes and nitrite negative.
  • The laboratory's microscopy, signed at 21:04: "No erythrocytes seen."
  • A dipstick positive for blood does not prove there are red cells. With no red cells on microscopy it suggests haemoglobinuria: haemoglobin spilled from red cells destroyed in the circulation.
  • A caution: TTP can also cause true blood in the urine. Microscopy tells you which you have.
  • When you inherit a diagnosis, ask what the symptoms actually were.
The decision: is a low count here a bleeding risk?9 of 17

TTP is a missing pair of scissors.

  • Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is a long, sticky protein that catches platelets. An enzyme called ADAMTS13 cuts it down to size.
  • In immune TTP, the commonest kind, an antibody knocks out ADAMTS13. The uncut VWF grabs platelets, and small vessels fill with platelet-rich clots.
  • One cause, three results. Platelets are used up: the low count. Red cells are sheared squeezing past: the fragments. Organs downstream are starved: mainly brain, heart and kidney.
  • These clots are platelets and VWF, not the clotting cascade. So the clotting screen is typically normal. Hers: INR (international normalised ratio) 1.0, fibrinogen 3.4 g/L.
  • Her count of 18 means clot, not bleeding. That is why the ladder does not apply.
The decision: would you have thought of it?10 of 17

It rarely looks like the textbook.

  • The textbook "pentad" (fever, neurological signs, kidney impairment, haemolysis, low platelets) dates from the 1960s. Fewer than 1 in 10 patients have all five, and organ involvement is not needed for the diagnosis.
  • Brain, 70 to 80%. Confusion, headache, seizures, speech disturbance, "often flitting and variable".
  • Heart. Troponin is raised in more than half, and a raised troponin carried a six-fold higher mortality (12.1% against 2%). Lauren's was 42 ng/L (normal below 14).
  • Kidney. Impairment is usually modest; dialysis is rare. Her creatinine: 118 µmol/L, from 64.
  • Her observation chart barely noticed: a national early warning score (NEWS2) of 4. Her thinking did: an abbreviated mental test score of 6 out of 10.

22:00 · a district general hospital13 of 17

Your job is four things, and none of them is the cure.

  • BSH: plasma exchange "within four hours but certainly by eight hours of a suspected diagnosis". Its graded recommendation (1A): "within four to eight hours".
  • Every case in England goes to a designated regional centre. So tonight:
  • 1. Ring. Haematology and your regional TTP centre (check your switchboard), now.
  • 2. Samples before any plasma goes in. ADAMTS13 activity and antibody. Group and antibody screen. HIV, hepatitis B and C.
  • 3. Review before she travels, by anaesthetics or critical care, with an airway escort where possible.
  • 4. Blue light. The centre thaws plasma while she is on the road.
  • Two things do not hold her up: the ADAMTS13 result, and a scan.
  • Why the hurry: historically about 9 in 10 died untreated; with exchange, 1 or 2 in 10. In one UK registry, most who died did so before treatment had started.
The decision: is a bag of plasma the same thing?14 of 17

Plasma exchange takes the antibody out and puts the enzyme back.

  • Her plasma, carrying the antibody, is removed. Donor plasma, carrying working ADAMTS13, replaces it: daily, starting at one and a half plasma volumes, until the platelets stay above 150.
  • Plasma infusion is not plasma exchange. Hanging plasma (fresh frozen plasma, FFP) is the lesser treatment. In the 1991 trial, 8 of 51 died by day nine on infusion, against 2 of 51 on exchange. It is a holding measure only: when exchange cannot be had, transfer is unavoidably delayed, and the centre advises it.
  • The centre adds three drugs. A steroid, because the cause is an antibody: methylprednisolone 1 g a day for three days, or prednisolone 1 mg/kg by mouth. Caplacizumab, which stops VWF catching platelets (NICE TA667). Rituximab within three days.
  • Recovery is counted in days, not hours. It relapses in 30 to 50%, so follow-up is lifelong.

Take home

  1. A low platelet count: is it real, is it alone, and why?
  2. Low platelets with anaemia: ask for the film. Fragments and haemolysis with a negative DAT: ring haematology tonight.
  3. In TTP the low count means clot. Avoid platelets, even for the line.
  4. Send ADAMTS13 first; never wait for it. Exchange ideally within four hours, certainly by eight.
  5. From a district hospital: the call, the samples, the review, the blue light.
