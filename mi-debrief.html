  • This page is about that condition: what it is, how to find it, how to treat it, and what to do when it turns up looking like a heart attack.
What it is2 / 43

Endocarditis is an infection growing on a heart valve.

  • The heart's valves are thin flaps that keep blood moving one way. Bacteria from the blood can settle on one and grow into a clump. The clump is called a vegetation.
  • “Infective endocarditis” means infection of the lining of the heart, and in practice of its valves, natural or artificial, or of a pacemaker lead.
  • It is uncommon: about 14 people in every 100,000 each year (a global estimate for 2019).
  • It is dangerous: about 1 in 6 die in hospital, 1 in 4 within a year, and more than a third within five years. The in-hospital figure has barely moved in twenty years.
How you would say it to a relative“He has an infection on one of the valves inside his heart. It is damaging the valve, and bits of it are breaking off and travelling round his body. He needs weeks of antibiotics into a vein, and he may need an operation on the valve.”
Who gets it3 / 43

Three things have to meet: a surface, bacteria in the blood, and a body that fails to clear them.

  • High risk: a previous episode of endocarditis; an artificial or repaired valve; some forms of heart disease from birth; a ventricular assist device (a mechanical heart pump).
  • Intermediate risk: rheumatic or wear-and-tear valve disease; a two-leaflet (bicuspid) aortic valve; a pacemaker or defibrillator; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (a thickened heart muscle).
  • Outside the heart: a line in a central vein, injecting drugs, a weakened immune system, dialysis, a recent stay in hospital, and a recent dental or surgical procedure.
  • Most cases are on the patient's own valve: 72% in one large international study, and about two-thirds of all cases start outside hospital.
  • Of people who get it on their own valve, about 30 to 40 in 100 had no known valve disease beforehand.
  • Marcus had no known heart problem and had never injected. He had had a molar taken out five weeks earlier.
Who gets it4 / 43

The mouth is the commonest door. The organism depends on where it came in.

  • Bacteria reach the blood through the mouth, the skin, the gut, the urinary tract, a needle or a hospital procedure.
  • In the mouth, extractions and any work on the gum or the root of a tooth carry the highest risk. But brushing, flossing and chewing put small numbers of bacteria into the blood every day, and may matter more in total than the occasional procedure.
  • Which organism (2,781 patients, 25 countries): Staphylococcus aureus 31%, mouth streptococci (the “viridans” group) 17%, coagulase-negative staphylococci 11%, enterococci 10%, nothing grown 10%.
  • Staphylococci lead overall. After dental work, on a natural valve, in the community, a mouth streptococcus is still the likely one. It is also the kinder one: about half the odds of dying in hospital. Staphylococcus aureus carries about one and a half times the odds.
  • In Marcus the organism is presumed to be a mouth streptococcus. In the case his cultures were sent and never came back, as on a real first evening.
  • Nobody asked him about his teeth. The extraction was found by looking in his mouth.
What is going wrong5 / 43

The clump is a shelter. Bacteria inside it are hard to reach and slow to die.

  1. A healthy valve lining resists bacteria.
  2. The lining is injured, by turbulent blood flow, wear or inflammation. Platelets (the blood's clotting cells) and fibrin (the mesh of a clot) stick to the raw patch. This first clot has no bacteria in it.
  3. Bacteria passing in the blood stick to that clot.
  4. More platelets and fibrin are laid over them. The bacteria multiply inside, in a layer that antibiotics and white cells penetrate poorly.
  • Inside the vegetation the bacteria grow slowly or lie dormant. A laboratory plate may call them “sensitive” to an antibiotic, and yet the antibiotic does not kill them.
  • That is why treatment lasts for weeks, and why drugs that kill bacteria are preferred to drugs that only hold them back.
  • Treatment has two phases: about two weeks to kill the bacteria that are growing freely, then a longer phase to deal with the dormant ones.
What is going wrong6 / 43

It wrecks the valve, it burrows around it, and it throws pieces.

  • It wrecks the valve. A leaflet is perforated or torn and the valve leaks. The heart fails. Heart failure is the commonest complication and the main reason for urgent surgery.
  • It burrows. In 10 to 30 in 100 natural-valve cases the infection spreads into the tissue around the valve as an abscess, most often at the aortic valve. A new delay in conduction on the ECG is a warning that this has happened.
  • It throws pieces. Fragments break off and block arteries elsewhere. This is embolism. It affects 20 to 50 in 100.
  • And the immune system joins in. Immune reactions can inflame the kidney's filters and cause some of the skin and eye signs.
  • Marcus: a leaking aortic valve (moderate to severe), no abscess, and pieces in five organs.
What is going wrong7 / 43

One valve, five organs.

  • From a valve on the left side of the heart, a fragment can go wherever arterial blood goes.
  • The brain and the spleen are the commonest targets. Up to half of all emboli cause no symptoms at the time.
  • Spleen: infarcts (wedges of dead tissue) in about 1 in 5, usually silent.
  • Spine: infection of a disc and the bones beside it in 2 to 10 in 100.
  • Heart arteries: about 2 to 3 in 100.
  • Marcus: a heart artery (the infarct); the spine (infection at L4–L5, the back pain he came in with); the spleen and both kidneys (wedge-shaped infarcts on CT). His CT head was clear.

How it turns up8 / 43

Usually a fever and a murmur. Often something else entirely.

  • It can be sudden and septic, or slow: weeks of sweats, weight loss and feeling unwell, with little or no fever.
  • Fever in about 8 in 10 in one registry and more than 9 in 10 in another. A murmur (the sound of blood leaking through a damaged valve) in about two-thirds. A murmur known to be new in about half.
  • It can present as its own complication, and it mimics rheumatological, neurological and malignant disease.
  • Back and muscle pain in about 1 in 8.
  • Marcus told us about his back. He called his rigors “I get hot. Doesn't everyone.” It was Sophie who described the shaking, the sheets soaked “most nights for a month”, and the stone in weight he had lost.

How it turns up10 / 43

Six situations where the next thought should be “is this the valve?”

  1. A fever with a new leaking-valve murmur.
  2. A fever with no obvious source in someone with a valve problem or an artificial valve.
  3. A fever after a procedure that puts bacteria in the blood.
  4. A long story of sweats, weight loss and feeling unwell in someone with a valve problem.
  5. Any embolus nobody can explain: a stroke, or an abscess in the spleen, kidney, brain or spine with no cause.
  6. Blood cultures that keep coming back positive with no explanation.
  • These are the UK guideline's prompts to investigate. A stroke or a mini-stroke with a fever belongs on the same list.

The answer12 / 43

At least two sets of blood cultures, taken at different times, before the antibiotic. Then the antibiotic, inside the hour.

  • ESC 2023 (the European Society of Cardiology guideline): at least three sets, 30 minutes apart, before any antibiotic. 10 mL in each bottle, from a vein in the arm, not from a line.
  • BSAC (the British Society for Antimicrobial Chemotherapy, the UK guideline), for severe sepsis: two sets, at different times, within the hour, then start. A third within the hour adds nothing.
  • So: at least two sets at different times before the antibiotic, and the antibiotic within the hour. Three if you can spare the half-hours. The case followed ESC and marked three; BSAC would have accepted two for a man this sick.
  • It is separate times that show the bacteria are in the blood continuously. Separate arms are not what makes cultures count. Clean skin is what stops contamination.
  • You do not need to wait for a temperature spike. In endocarditis the bacteria are in the blood almost all the time.
  • Why it matters. Antibiotics given first are the commonest reason cultures grow nothing. In a study of 325 adults with severe sepsis, cultures were positive in 31 in 100 before the antibiotic and in 19 in 100 about an hour after it. (That was sepsis in general, not endocarditis.) In partly treated endocarditis, cultures may need 7 to 10 days off antibiotics before they turn positive.
  • Grow nothing and you are treating blind: no organism, no sensitivities, nothing to narrow to, for weeks of treatment.
  • If the patient is stable and not septic, BSAC goes further: wait for the culture results before starting anything.

How to prove it14 / 43

Scan through the chest wall first. Then, in almost everyone, scan from the gullet.

  • Transthoracic echo (TTE): an ultrasound probe on the front of the chest. The first test. It finds about 7 in 10 vegetations on a natural valve.
  • Transoesophageal echo (TOE): a probe passed down the gullet. The gullet runs directly behind the left atrium, one of the heart's chambers, with only the heart's lining sac between them. So the probe sits right behind the heart. It finds 9 in 10 or more, and is better for small vegetations, for an abscess around the valve, and for artificial valves.
  • ESC 2023: TOE for everyone whose TTE is negative or unclear and in whom you still suspect it. And, new in 2023, TOE even when the TTE is positive, to look for complications.
  • Do the first echo promptly (BSAC: within 24 hours). If the echo is negative and you still suspect it, repeat in 5 to 7 days.
  • Marcus's TTE, done on a trolley in a breathless man: a leaking aortic valve, and “not diagnostic for vegetations at this image quality”.
  • Marcus's TOE: a 12 mm mobile vegetation on the aortic valve, a second smaller one, moderate to severe leak, no abscess, and the front wall of the heart moving poorly.
  • A normal TTE is not the answer. Escalate the imaging, not the reassurance.
How to prove it15 / 43

The ESC 2023 criteria: two big things and five small ones.

Major

  1. Blood cultures. A typical organism in two separate cultures (mouth streptococci, Streptococcus gallolyticus, the HACEK group, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecalis). Or cultures that are continuously positive: two or more drawn more than 12 hours apart; or all of three, or most of four or more, with the first and last at least an hour apart.
  2. Imaging. A lesion typical of endocarditis on or around a valve, on echo, cardiac CT or a nuclear scan.

Minor

  1. A heart condition that carries risk, or injecting drugs.
  2. Fever above 38 °C.
  3. Emboli and their effects, including ones found only on a scan, and including infection of the spine.
  4. Immune signs: kidney inflammation (glomerulonephritis), Osler nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor.
  5. A positive culture that falls short of a major criterion.

Adding up

  • Definite: 2 major. Or 1 major and at least 3 minor. Or 5 minor.
  • Possible: 1 major and 1 or 2 minor. Or 3 or 4 minor.
  • Rejected: neither.
  • Everything inside one minor criterion counts once. Three emboli are one criterion, not three.
  • Criteria help you classify. They do not replace judgement, and they are weakest when cultures are negative, too few sets were sent, or the valve is artificial.
How to prove it16 / 43

“The Duke criteria”: there are now two 2023 sets. The one from the year 2000 is retired.

  • Modified Duke, 2000. The version in most revision notes and in the 2012 UK guideline. It picks up about 8 in 10. Superseded.
  • ESC 2023. The European set, on the previous card. This case was marked against ESC.
  • Duke-ISCVID 2023. An international set.
Three differences that matter
  1. Cultures. Duke-ISCVID dropped the rules about timing and separate needle-sticks.
  2. A third major criterion. Duke-ISCVID adds endocarditis seen directly at operation.
  3. The spine. ESC names infection of the spine, and emboli seen only on a scan, in its minor criteria.
  • Both new sets pick up more cases than the old one. Duke-ISCVID seems to make fewer false diagnoses. ESC says its own set still needs testing in new patients.

The answer20 / 43

The infected clump is on the aortic valve. The heart's own arteries open just above it.

  • The aortic valve is the exit from the heart. It has three cusps (flaps): the right coronary, the left coronary and the non-coronary.
  • Just above the valve the wall of the aorta has three pockets. Two of them give rise to the coronary arteries, right and left, which supply the heart muscle. The third gives rise to no artery.
  • So a vegetation on the aortic valve is moving beside the openings of the heart's own arteries.
  • That closeness is the stated reason why, when endocarditis blocks a heart artery, it is usually the aortic valve that is infected: 12 of 14 patients in one series.
  • Marcus: a 12 mm mobile vegetation on the non-coronary cusp, swinging into the heart as the valve closed, and a second, smaller one on the right coronary cusp.
The infarct that was an embolus21 / 43

A fragment comes loose, is carried into a heart artery, and jams.

  • A large, mobile vegetation is the kind that sheds. Size and mobility are what best predict an embolus.
  • In plain terms (our account): a piece that enters a coronary artery blocks it. The heart muscle beyond the blockage is starved of oxygen and starts to die. That is a heart attack: a myocardial infarction.
  • This is coronary embolism: an artery blocked by something that formed elsewhere and was carried in.
  • Which artery? In the largest series, as a later paper reports it, the pieces went to all three main heart arteries with no favourite.
  • In Marcus the case says it was the artery to the front wall of the heart. No angiogram was done in the case; his TOE showed the front wall moving poorly.
The infarct that was an embolus22 / 43

The heart muscle does not know what blocked its artery. The ECG looks exactly the same.

Our explanationNo guideline spells this out.
  • An ECG records the electricity of the heart muscle. When an artery is blocked, the starved muscle changes its signal, and the tracing shows ST elevation.
  • The ECG is reading the muscle. It has no view of the artery.
  • A clot on a plaque and a piece of infected valve starve the muscle in the same way. So the tracing is the same.
  • The tracing tells you an artery is blocked. It cannot tell you what the plug is made of.
  • What differs is the cause, and with it almost everything you do next.
  • It is still a real heart attack. His troponin rose, his tracing changed and the front wall of his heart stopped moving properly.
The infarct that was an embolus23 / 43

A piece in the artery is the commonest. There are four others.

  1. A piece blocks the artery (embolism). 23 of 26 patients in the largest series.
  2. An abscess beside the valve squeezes the artery from outside. Commonest in an older series in which half the patients had artificial valves.
  3. A large vegetation sits over the opening of the artery and plugs it.
  4. The valve leaks so badly that the pressure feeding the heart arteries falls.
  5. Sepsis asks more of a heart whose arteries were already narrowed.
  • How often: a heart artery event in about 2 to 3 of every 100 people with endocarditis.
  • When: early. In published cases of ST elevation the heart attack was the first sign of endocarditis in 6 of 10.
  • What follows: heart failure in about three-quarters, and roughly double the risk of dying.
  • How good is this evidence? One series of 26 patients, one of 14, and a review of 100 published case reports.
Our caution, not a measured factSilent and fatal events are likely to be missed, and case reports favour the dramatic.

The answer27 / 43

No. In endocarditis the clot-dissolving drug causes major bleeding.

  • ESC 2023: thrombolysis is not recommended in patients with endocarditis. Class III.
  • What the grade means, in ESC's words. Class III: evidence or general agreement that a treatment is not useful or effective, and may be harmful. Level C: the consensus of experts, or small studies, retrospective studies and registries.
  • Why. It is thought to be because the same valve has been seeding the brain. Silent small strokes are found in up to 8 in 10 on MRI. Infective (mycotic) aneurysms, places where infection in an artery's wall has made it balloon, most often in the brain, are found in roughly 1 to 3 in 10 of those imaged. They bleed, particularly on blood-thinning drugs.
  • In strokes caused by endocarditis: bleeding into the brain after thrombolysis in 20 in 100, against 6.5 in 100 without endocarditis.
  • In heart attacks caused by endocarditis: eleven published patients were given it. There were nine major bleeds.
  • How strong is this? Not strong. The heart evidence is eleven case reports. The rest is borrowed from stroke. It all points the same way.
  • One wrinkle: the European heart attack guideline lists endocarditis as a “relative” contraindication. The endocarditis guideline is the specific one, and it says no.
The infarct that was an embolus28 / 43

The reflex heart-attack drugs are built for a plaque. He does not have one.

  • Aspirin. A loading dose given before the diagnosis is known is understandable. Once it is endocarditis, aspirin has no proven role. In the one trial (115 patients), four weeks of aspirin did not reduce emboli (28.3% against 20.0% on placebo) and there was a trend to more bleeding.
  • A second anti-platelet drug. No guideline we could find makes a recommendation. The reasoning against it: no plaque in 95 in 100 published cases, aspirin did not help, and ESC advises sparing use of anti-platelet and blood-thinning drugs around valve surgery.
  • Full-dose blood thinner (anticoagulation). Do not start one for the embolus. No benefit has been shown, and aneurysms bleed more on it.
  • Already on a blood thinner for another reason? ESC continues it unless there is bleeding, and swaps it for heparin early if surgery is likely.
  • The trap. A heart artery blocked by a clot from a fibrillating heart is treated with a blood thinner. The same blockage from an infected valve is not. Opposite advice. That is why you must find where the embolus came from.
The infarct that was an embolus29 / 43

He may still need his artery opened. But the catheter has to pass the infected valve to get there.

  • There are no trials. ESC's endocarditis guideline makes no recommendation on a blocked heart artery. Published authors say management “cannot be standardized”.
  • The warning. ESC: vegetations on the aortic valve may rule out a catheter study of the heart arteries, because the catheter can knock pieces off. It has been done safely in some studies, particularly when the vegetation is not very large and mobile. His was 12 mm and mobile. (ESC wrote this about checking the arteries before valve surgery. We are applying it here.)
  • If the lab is used: operators generally try to suck the embolus out (aspiration) and avoid leaving a stent in infected material. The worry about a stent is infection of the metal and a weak spot in the artery wall. That worry is argued, not measured.
  • Suction is not an exotic idea: NICE lists it as something to consider in ordinary angioplasty for a heart attack.
  • What the published cases show (success means the artery reopened, nothing more): suction 21 of 31, balloon 9 of 16, stent 14 of 16. The numbers do not show suction to be better.
  • So it is a team decision: the interventional cardiologist and the Endocarditis Team, together, with the echo in front of them.

The infarct that was an embolus30 / 43

The blocked artery is the consequence. The valve is the cause, and the valve is what you treat.

  • Whatever happens to the artery, the source is still there and still shedding. The treatment is the treatment of endocarditis: cultures, antibiotics, a picture of the valve, and the surgical team.
  • A coronary embolus counts as an embolic event when the surgeons weigh up operating (see the embolus rule, below). The heart attack often brings heart failure, which is itself the leading reason for surgery.
  • Removing the embolus from the artery during the valve operation has been reported.
  • What nobody knows: whether a fresh heart attack should delay valve surgery, and by how long. No guideline says.
The evidence ladder
  • Randomised trials of how to manage this: none.
  • Guideline recommendations on the blocked artery: none. One firm line: no thrombolysis.
  • Case series: two, of 26 and 14 patients.
  • Case reports: 100, gathered in one review.
  • Everything beyond “do not thrombolyse” is expert opinion. Say so when you are asked.
The spine31 / 43

New back pain with a fever is an infected spine until a scan says otherwise.

  • Bacteria from the valve can settle in a disc and the bones beside it. This is spondylodiscitis, also called vertebral osteomyelitis. It complicates 2 to 10 in 100 cases of endocarditis.
  • An X-ray cannot rule it out. Bone destruction takes 3 to 6 weeks to show on a plain film.
  • MRI is the test. It is accurate in about 9 in 10. Done very early, even MRI can be falsely normal.
  • How much of the spine? No guideline we read requires a scan of the whole spine. The argument for one: in a series of 82 people thought to have infection at a single level, a whole-spine scan found a second, separate site in about 1 in 5.
  • Examine the legs and write it down: power and sensation. That is how you will know if anything changes.
  • Marcus: pain low in the back that woke him at three each morning. Focal tenderness at L4–L5. ESR 88. X-ray “within normal limits for age”. MRI: infection of the L4–L5 disc and the bones either side, a little inflammation in front of the spinal canal, no pressure on the nerves, nothing to drain, and no second site.
  • Keep it in proportion: among people with endocarditis and back pain, only about 1 in 25 turn out to have an infected spine.
The spine32 / 43

An infected spine with no obvious cause should send you to the heart.

  • Seen from the valve, the spine is involved in 2 to 10 in 100.
  • Seen from the spine, it is far commoner: endocarditis is behind 20 to 30 in 100 cases of spontaneous spinal infection. In one series six such patients had no sign of it at all and were found only because someone did an echo.
  • ESC: do an echo in spinal infection when the blood cultures grow a typical organism. And in endocarditis with a suspect back, do an MRI.
  • What it changes. The antibiotics for the valve treat the spine too, but for longer: the longer end of the range, and more than six weeks for difficult organisms or severe destruction.
  • What it does not change. An infected spine is not a reason to hold off urgent heart surgery.
  • The spinal surgeons are for a new weakness, numbness, a change in bladder or bowel, or an unstable spine.
How to treat it33 / 43

Before the organism is known: cover the three likely families, into a vein, and ring microbiology.

  • The first regimen has to cover staphylococci, streptococci and enterococci. It is narrowed once the organism is known, usually within a day or two.
  • No penicillin allergy (ESC): ampicillin with ceftriaxone, or ampicillin with flucloxacillin and gentamicin.
  • Penicillin allergy (ESC and BSAC): vancomycin with gentamicin. BSAC: if the kidneys are a concern, ciprofloxacin in place of gentamicin.
  • So for Marcus: vancomycin plus a second agent on microbiology's advice. His creatinine had risen from 84 to 128, which is BSAC's stated reason to avoid gentamicin.
  • Vancomycin and gentamicin both need blood levels and both can harm the kidneys. BSAC warns that together they are worse.
  • How weak is this? ESC's own recommendation for the allergy regimen is Class IIb, level C.
  • The allergy. A childhood rash on its own is often not a true allergy. Swelling of the face or throat, hives, wheeze or collapse within about an hour of a dose is a different matter: that is an immediate reaction, and it rules out penicillins and cephalosporins. Marcus's mother says he swelled up, so tonight the label stands and he gets vancomycin. It is still worth an allergy specialist's opinion during the admission, because if penicillin turns out to be safe it is the better drug.

How to treat it34 / 43

Four weeks for a mouth streptococcus on a natural valve. Longer if the spine is infected.

  • Mouth streptococcus sensitive to penicillin, natural valve: penicillin, amoxicillin or ceftriaxone into a vein for 4 weeks. Cure is expected in more than 95 in 100.
  • Penicillin allergy: vancomycin for 4 weeks.
  • With an infected spine: the longer course. That is why “six weeks” was the figure in the case. It is not the figure for everyone.
  • The clock starts on the first day of effective treatment, not the day of any operation.
  • Tablets. In stable patients, after at least 10 days into a vein, finishing the course by mouth was no worse than staying on the drip (the POET trial, 400 patients). ESC: this should be considered. ESC asks for a combination of two tablets; which ones is in a table we could not read, so none is named here. The 2012 UK guideline says not to switch; on this point it is out of date.
  • Stable, in the trial's flowchart, means: no fever for more than two days, CRP under a quarter of its peak or under 20, and a TOE showing no abscess and nothing that needs an operation.
How to treat it35 / 43

Four things the case stopped you doing, and one it got wrong.

These are not about endocarditis. They are about a septic man with a systolic blood pressure of 88, a leaking aortic valve and a heart attack in progress.

  1. Fluid. NICE: 250 mL at a time, up to a litre, looking at him between each; a leaking valve is a reason to be stricter still. One review of valve emergencies advises fluid only with clear signs of low volume. Be aware that the international sepsis guideline still suggests a much larger volume (at least 30 mL/kg within three hours), on weak evidence.
  2. GTN (glyceryl trinitrate, a nitrate drip). Its licence lists shock and uncorrected low blood volume as reasons not to give it, and says a systolic pressure below 90 must be avoided. His was 88.
  3. A beta-blocker into a vein. The licence for intravenous metoprolol lists low blood pressure as a reason not to give it, and says no further doses below a systolic of 90.
  4. Anti-sickness drugs. Ondansetron is a poor choice on a long QT interval: it lengthens the QT further, and his was already long (QTc 476 ms on the arrival tracing). But no anti-sickness drug here is risk-free. Metoclopramide carries a QT warning too. Whichever is used: the smallest dose, given slowly, on a monitor, with potassium and magnesium corrected.
  5. Oxygen. No routine oxygen; aim for 94 to 98% (NICE). The case used 90%. His saturation on arrival was 94% on air.
When the surgeons take the valve36 / 43

Heart failure. Infection that will not come under control. Stopping the next embolus.

  • In one large registry surgery was indicated in 69% of people with endocarditis. Not operating when it was indicated was an independent predictor of death.
  • 1. Heart failure. The valve has failed. With fluid on the lungs that will not clear, or shock: emergency.
  • 2. Uncontrolled infection. An abscess around the valve, a vegetation that is growing, or a new heart block: urgent. Cultures still positive after more than a week on the right antibiotic: surgery should be considered.
  • 3. Preventing embolism. The next card.
  • The words mean something. Emergency: within 24 hours. Urgent: within 3 to 5 days. Non-urgent: within the same admission.
  • What to watch for on the ward (ESC). A fever that will not settle, a new heart block on the ECG, chest pain, a new murmur, another embolus or heart failure may mean the infection has spread around the valve. Breathlessness and fluid on the lungs are how heart failure shows.
  • Surgery carries its own risk: 10 to 20 in 100 die around the operation.

Who to ring38 / 43

Complicated endocarditis is managed in a Heart Valve Centre, early. Marcus was complicated.

  • ESC 2023, Class I: complicated endocarditis should be diagnosed and managed at an early stage in a Heart Valve Centre.
  • A Heart Valve Centre, in ESC's words, has all the diagnostic and treatment resources for endocarditis, immediate surgical facilities, and an Endocarditis Team whose core is cardiologists, heart surgeons, infection specialists and microbiologists. A “referring centre” is a hospital without a heart surgery team.
  • “Keep in early and regular touch with the centre” is the rule for uncomplicated disease only.
  • Complicated means any of: an embolus; a stroke; a severely leaking valve; an abscess; unstable circulation needing support; an artificial valve or an infected device; cultures still positive after a week; an aggressive organism. Marcus had emboli.
In a hospital without heart surgery, ring
  1. Microbiology or infectious diseases.
  2. Cardiology, for the echo.

Prevention39 / 43

Would antibiotics before the extraction have stopped this? Not under either rulebook. From now on, the answer changes.

  • NICE (UK): antibiotics to prevent endocarditis are not recommended routinely for dental procedures. For people at high risk, NICE points dentists to Scottish dental guidance.
  • ESC (Europe): antibiotics are recommended for high-risk patients before extractions and surgery on the gums or the roots of teeth. The dose is amoxicillin 2 g by mouth, 30 to 60 minutes before. Clindamycin is no longer recommended.
  • This is a real disagreement. ESC itself concedes that no randomised trial exists.
  • Marcus before: no known valve problem. Neither NICE nor ESC would have given him anything.
  • Marcus now: he has had endocarditis. That puts him in the high-risk group from now on. Under ESC he should have antibiotics before an extraction. With his penicillin history, the options are azithromycin, clarithromycin or doxycycline.
  • For everyone at risk (ESC): brush twice a day, see a dentist regularly, look after the skin, no self-prescribed antibiotics, and avoid piercings and tattoos.
  • NICE: tell patients the symptoms of endocarditis and when to seek help.

Take home42 / 43

Five things to carry onto the ward.

  1. A fever with a murmur, or an embolus nobody can explain: think of the valve. The hand signs are rare, and their absence means nothing.
  2. Blood cultures before the antibiotic: at least two sets at different times, and the antibiotic within the hour.
  3. Echo through the chest first, then from the gullet in almost everyone. A normal first echo misses 3 in 10.
  4. ST elevation with a fever and a murmur may be a piece of the valve. No thrombolysis. Talk to cardiology and the Endocarditis Team before the cath lab. Treat the valve.
  5. Emboli, a severely leaking valve, an abscess: that is complicated disease. Ring the Heart Valve Centre early. The surgeons operate for heart failure, uncontrolled infection and to stop the next embolus.
